The Nature of Electromagnetism

Four fundamental forces govern the physical universe. Electromagnetism is the one we engineer with: it acts between charges, extends to infinity, and — through fields that change in time — carries energy and information as waves.

Strongest

Nuclear force

Binds protons and neutrons, but acts only over nuclear distances (~10⁻¹⁵ m).

relative strength ~ $1$
Dominant at atomic scale

Electromagnetic force

Acts between all charged particles; holds atoms, molecules and solids together.

relative strength ~ $10^{-2}$
Radioactivity

Weak interaction

Responsible for radioactive β-decay of nuclei and particles.

relative strength ~ $10^{-14}$
Dominant at cosmic scale

Gravitational force

By far the weakest, but it is always attractive and adds up over huge masses — it rules planets and galaxies.

relative strength ~ $10^{-41}$

Gravity versus the Electric Force

Newton's law of gravitation and Coulomb's law have exactly the same $1/R^2$ form. Pick a pair of particles and a separation: the ratio $F_e/F_g$ does not depend on distance at all.

$$\mathbf{F}_{g_{21}} = -\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{12}\,\frac{G m_1 m_2}{R_{12}^2}\ \ (\mathrm{N})$$
$$\mathbf{F}_{e_{21}} = \hat{\mathbf{R}}_{12}\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_{12}^2}\ \ (\mathrm{N})$$

Electric Field of a Point Charge

The field is the force per unit test charge. Move the test charge $q'$: the field $\mathbf{E}$ at its position is the same whatever $q'$ is, but the force $\mathbf{F} = q'\mathbf{E}$ reverses for a negative test charge.

$$\mathbf{E} = \hat{\mathbf{R}}\,\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\ \ (\mathrm{V/m}), \qquad \mathbf{F}_e = q'\mathbf{E}, \qquad \mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E}$$

Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative ones. Their density is proportional to $|\mathbf{E}|$ — halve the distance and the field is four times stronger.

Magnetic Field around a Straight Wire

A current is a flow of charge, and moving charges produce a magnetic field. For an infinitely long wire the field circles the wire (right-hand rule) and falls as $1/r$. Unlike charges, magnetic poles always come in pairs.

$$\mathbf{B} = \boldsymbol{\hat{\phi}}\,\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\ \ (\mathrm{T}), \qquad \mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}, \qquad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}$$

Electrodynamics

Static and Dynamic Fields

$\mathbf{E}$ is produced by charges $q$ and $\mathbf{B}$ by currents $I = dq/dt$. Whether the two fields are coupled depends on whether they change in time: a time-varying $\mathbf{E}$ generates $\mathbf{B}$ and vice versa.

Static

Does not change with time; "dc" is a synonym.

Dynamic

Varies with time, no pattern implied.

Waveform

A plot of a quantity versus time.

Periodic

Repeats every period $T$ (sinusoid, square wave…).

Sinusoidal

"ac": varies as $\sin$ or $\cos$ of time — the basis of phasor analysis.

The three branches of electromagnetics

Electrostatics

Stationary charges → static $\mathbf{E}$, no $\mathbf{B}$. Maxwell: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_{\mathrm{v}}$, $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$.

Magnetostatics

Steady (dc) currents → static $\mathbf{B}$. Maxwell: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$, $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$.

Electromagnetics

Time-varying $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ coupled together — waves, antennas, induction.

Material properties

Constitutive Parameters of Materials

Three parameters describe how a material responds to electric and magnetic fields.

Electric

Permittivity $\varepsilon$ (F/m)

Relates electric flux density to electric field. $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$, with $\varepsilon_0 \approx 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m.

$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E}$$
Magnetic

Permeability $\mu$ (H/m)

Relates magnetic flux density to magnetic field intensity. $\mu = \mu_r\mu_0$, with $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m.

$$\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$$
Conduction

Conductivity $\sigma$ (S/m)

How freely charges move: $\sigma = 0$ perfect dielectric, $\sigma = \infty$ perfect conductor. Copper: $5.8\times10^{7}$ S/m.

$$\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$$
