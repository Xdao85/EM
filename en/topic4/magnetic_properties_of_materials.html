Magnetic Properties of Materials

Magnetostatics · Magnetic Materials

Where Magnetism in Matter Comes From

Electrons carry magnetic moments associated with orbital motion and intrinsic spin. Spin is a quantum property, not literal rotation of a small charged sphere. Atomic moments may cancel or combine depending on the material. An applied $\mathbf{H}$ changes their response, producing magnetization $\mathbf{M}$, the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume.

1. Atomic Moments

Orbit and spin

$$m_{\mathrm{o}} = -\frac{e}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}L_{\mathrm{e}},\qquad |m_{\mathrm{s}}| = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}$$

The Bohr magneton, $\mu_{\mathrm{B}} = 9.27\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$, is the natural scale for electron magnetic moments. A spin projection contributes approximately one Bohr magneton in magnitude; an atom's total moment depends on its quantum state and on how orbital and spin contributions combine.

2. Magnetization

Linear materials

$$\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M}),\qquad \mathbf{M} = \chi_{\mathrm{m}}\mathbf{H}$$

So $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ with $\mu = \mu_0(1 + \chi_{\mathrm{m}})$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{r}} = 1 + \chi_{\mathrm{m}}$.

3. Ferromagnets

Domains and hysteresis

$$B = B(H,\ \text{history})$$

Domains of aligned moments grow and rotate as $H$ changes. The response depends on the past, which gives the $B$–$H$ hysteresis loop. Remanence is the response remaining when the applied field returns to zero; coercivity is the opposing field needed to bring the flux density back to zero. These quantities distinguish soft magnetic cores from permanent-magnet materials.

An Orbiting Electron Is a Current Loop

An electron (charge $-e$) moving at speed $u$ on a circle of radius $r$ is a current $I = -eu/2\pi r$ around the area $\pi r^2$. Its moment $m_{\mathrm{o}} = IA$ is tied to its angular momentum $L_{\mathrm{e}} = m_{\mathrm{e}}ur$ by the fixed ratio $e/2m_{\mathrm{e}}$. Pick a Bohr orbit ($L_{\mathrm{e}} = n\hbar$) or set $r$ and $u$ freely: the ratio never changes.

Worked exercise

Saturation magnetization of iron

Iron has about $8.5\times10^{28}$ atoms/m³. If every atom contributes aligned spin moments of $k\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$ each, the magnetization is $M = N k\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$ and the extra flux density is $\mu_0 M$. The textbook exercise takes $k = 1$; the measured value for iron corresponds to $k \approx 2.2$.

$$M = N\,k\,\frac{e\hbar}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}} = (8.5\times10^{28})(9.27\times10^{-24}) \approx 7.9\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{A/m}\quad(k = 1)$$

Dia-, Para- and Ferromagnets in a Uniform Field

A sphere of permeability $\mu_{\mathrm{r}}$ is placed in a uniform field $\mathbf{B}_0$. The exact solution: inside, $\mathbf{H}$ is uniform and equal to $\frac{3}{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}+2}\mathbf{H}_0$; outside, the magnetized sphere adds a dipole field. The lines are the exact $\mathbf{B}$ lines (equal flux between neighbors). A ferromagnet draws flux in; a diamagnet pushes it out; a superconductor ($\mu_{\mathrm{r}} \to 0$) expels it completely.

Domains and the Hysteresis Loop

The sample is a grid of small grains, each with its own easy axis (thin lines mark grain boundaries). Every cell turns its moment to a local energy minimum: anisotropy pulls it toward the easy axis, the applied $H$ (along $x$) pulls it toward $+x$, and exchange coupling pulls it toward its neighbors. When a minimum disappears the moment flips, and that is where hysteresis comes from. Start from the demagnetized state, raise $H$ to trace the initial curve, then sweep back and forth. Sweep the applied field through a full cycle and compare the forward and return branches. The loop area represents energy dissipated per unit volume per cycle; this is why hysteresis matters in AC magnetic cores.
