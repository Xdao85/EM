Where the Energy of an Inductor Lives

Building up a current in an inductor takes work against the induced voltage $v = L\,di/dt$. That work is stored, and it can be written either with circuit quantities ($L$, $I$) or as an energy density spread through the magnetic field. The energy formula assumes constant inductance and a linear material. A current change transfers energy into or out of the magnetic field; a steady ideal inductor stores energy without continually consuming power.

1. Circuit View

Energy in an inductor

$$W_{\mathrm{m}} = \int_0^I L\,i\,di = \tfrac12 LI^2$$

Quadratic in current: doubling $I$ quadruples the stored energy.

2. Field View

Energy density

$$w_{\mathrm{m}} = \tfrac12\mu H^2 = \tfrac12\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{H} = \frac{B^2}{2\mu}\quad(\mathrm{J/m^3})$$

For the same $B$, a region of low $\mu$ (air) stores far more energy than a high-$\mu$ core.

3. Total

Integrate over space

$$W_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac12\int_v \mu H^2\,dv$$

For a long solenoid this reproduces $\frac12LI^2$ exactly, as the second simulation checks.

Interactive Simulation 1

Energizing and Discharging an RL Circuit

Close the switch: the current rises as $i(t) = I_\infty(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau = L/R$, and the stored energy $\tfrac12Li^2$ rises with it. Open it (the inductor then discharges through $R$): the energy flows back out and is dissipated. Time is slowed so that one run of $6\tau$ takes about 4 s. The current cannot change instantaneously with a finite inductor voltage. Watch the initial rapid rise slow toward the final value, and distinguish the magnetic energy from the heat dissipated in the resistor.

Circuit Energy = Field Energy for a Solenoid

For a long solenoid $L = \mu N^2S/l$ and $H = NI/l$ inside. Substituting shows $\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12\mu H^2(lS)$: the energy is the energy density times the interior volume. Both numbers are computed independently below.

Interactive Simulation 3

Energy in an Air Gap

An iron ring (mean length $l_c$, $\mu_r$) with a thin air gap $g$ is wound with $N$ turns. $B$ is continuous across the gap faces ($B_{\mathrm{n}}$ continuity), so the same $B$ exists in iron and air, but $w_{\mathrm{m}} = B^2/2\mu$ is $\mu_r$ times larger in the air. A gap of a millimeter can hold most of the energy. Compare equal flux density, not equal magnetic field intensity, in the core and gap. This distinction explains why an air gap can store much of an inductor's energy despite its small volume.
