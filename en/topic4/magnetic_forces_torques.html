Forces on Moving Charges, Currents and Loops

A charge moving with velocity $\mathbf{u}$ in a magnetic field feels a force perpendicular to its motion. Summed over the charges of a current, this becomes the force on a wire; applied to a closed loop it produces no net force in a uniform field but a torque that turns motors. Use the right-hand rule for positive charge and reverse the result for negative charge. A magnetic field changes a free particle's direction, while electric fields and an external power supply account for energy transfer in a working motor.

1. Charge

Lorentz force

$$\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{E} + q\,\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B}$$

$\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{m}}\perp\mathbf{u}$, so the magnetic force changes only the direction of motion and does no work.

2. Current

Force on a conductor

$$\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{m}} = I\int_C d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}$$

In a uniform field $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{m}} = I\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\mathbf{B}$, where $\boldsymbol{\ell}$ joins the ends; for a closed loop $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{m}} = 0$.

3. Loop

Magnetic torque

$$\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B},\quad \mathbf{m} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\,NIA$$

$\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ follows the right-hand rule. The torque tries to align $\mathbf{m}$ with $\mathbf{B}$.

A Charged Particle in E and B Fields

The motion is integrated with the Boris method, which keeps the speed exactly constant in a pure magnetic field. In a uniform $\mathbf{B}$ the particle circles with gyroradius $r_{\mathrm{g}} = mu_\perp/|q|B$ and angular frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{c}} = |q|B/m$; any velocity along $\mathbf{B}$ stretches the circle into a helix. In the magnetic bottle the field is the exact field of two coaxial coils: it is stronger at the coils, and a particle whose pitch angle is outside the loss cone is reflected back and forth.

Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A wire runs from $a$ to $b$ along different paths. The force $I\int d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}$ is summed numerically over the path. In a uniform field every path between the same endpoints gives the same force $I\boldsymbol{\ell}_{ab}\times\mathbf{B}$, and a closed loop gives zero: the forces on the straight and curved parts cancel (the semicircle example of Fawwaz T. Ulaby and Umberto Ravaioli).

Torque on a Rectangular Loop — the DC Motor

A rectangular loop ($N$ turns, sides $a \times b$) can turn about the $z$-axis in a uniform field $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}B_0$. Forces on the two sides parallel to the axis form a couple. Release the loop to watch it swing to $\mathbf{m}\parallel\mathbf{B}$; switch on the commutator (current reversed every half turn) and it keeps spinning like a DC motor. Zero net force does not mean zero torque: equal opposite forces on separated sides form a couple. Reverse the current and observe the torque direction reverse.
