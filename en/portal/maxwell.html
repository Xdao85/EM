Maxwell's equations

The four laws the whole course builds towards. Switch the form to see the same physics three ways, then follow the links to the modules where each law is developed. Read each law as a physical statement about sources, flux or circulation. Integral forms describe an entire region; differential forms describe local behavior; phasor forms describe sinusoidal steady state at a specified frequency.

Constitutive relations

The equations close only when the material is known. For a linear, isotropic medium: These constitutive relations connect the field intensities to material response; Maxwell's laws alone do not specify that response.

$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E},\qquad \mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H},\qquad \mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$$

In free space $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$, $\mu = \mu_0$, $\sigma = 0$, and the fields travel at $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$.

Static limit ($\partial/\partial t = 0$)

Without time variation the four laws split into two independent pairs, which is why Topics 3 and 4 can be studied separately: This separation concerns the field equations for stationary sources; both static fields can still exert forces on a moving charge.

Electrostatics
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_v,\quad \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$$
Magnetostatics
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0,\quad \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$$

Topic 5 brings back $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t$ and $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$, which couple $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ into waves.
