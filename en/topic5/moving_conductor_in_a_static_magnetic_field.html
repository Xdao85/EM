A Conductor Moving Through a Static Field

When a conductor moves through a stationary magnetic field, each charge carrier feels the magnetic force $q\,\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B}$. To the charges inside the conductor this looks exactly like an electric field, the motional field $\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{m}} = \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B}$, and its line integral is the motional emf. The motional field denotes force per unit positive charge due to motion; it is not an additional electrostatic field independent of velocity. Charge separation creates an actual electric field that can balance the magnetic force in an open rod.

1. Magnetic Force

On each charge

$$\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{m}} = q(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B})$$

Perpendicular to both the motion and the field.

2. Motional Field

Force per unit charge

$$\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{m}}}{q} = \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B}$$

Positive charges drift along $\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{m}}$ and pile up at one end of the conductor.

3. Motional emf

Line integral

$$V_{\mathrm{emf}}^{\mathrm{m}} = \oint_C(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l}$$

Only segments that cut across field lines contribute: a segment along $\mathbf{u}$, along $\mathbf{B}$, or perpendicular to $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B}$ gives zero.

Sliding Bar on Rails

A bar of length $l$ slides at speed $u$ on two rails closed by a resistor $R$ at $x = 0$. In the textbook example the field grows with $x$: $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}B_0x$. The line integral over the bar and the flux method $-d\Phi/dt$ give the same emf. The induced current also produces a force on the bar that opposes the motion; the mechanical power needed to keep it moving equals the power dissipated in $R$. The external pulling force supplies the dissipated electrical energy. The opposing magnetic force is the mechanical expression of Lenz's law, rather than a source of free energy.

Loop Moving Through a Decaying Field

A rectangular loop ($l = 2$ m along $x$, $w = 0.5$ m along $y$) moves in $+y$ at speed $u$ through $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}\,0.2e^{-0.1y}$ T. $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}uB(y)$, so only the two sides along $x$ contribute, and they nearly cancel: the net emf is $ul[B(y_1) - B(y_2)]$. At $y_1 = 2$ m, $u = 5$ m/s and $R = 5\,\Omega$ this gives the textbook current of about 16 mA.

Rod Moving Beside a Current-Carrying Wire

A long wire carries $I$ along $z$; a metal rod lying radially from $r_1$ to $r_2$ moves parallel to the wire at speed $u$. The field $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\,\mu_0I/2\pi r$ gives $\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{m}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}u\times\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}B = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}\,u\mu_0I/2\pi r$, pointing toward the wire, and the rod voltage grows with the logarithm of $r_2/r_1$.
