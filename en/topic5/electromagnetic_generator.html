Generators and Motors: One Machine, Two Directions

A loop turning in a magnetic field is both a generator and a motor. Turned by an engine, it produces a motional emf that drives current through a load. Fed with current, it feels a torque $\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}$ and turns. In an ideal conversion model, electrical and mechanical conversion powers match. Winding losses and changes in stored or kinetic energy must also be included when comparing input and output power.

1. Motional emf

Rotating loop

$$V_{\mathrm{emf}}^{\mathrm{m}} = \oint(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l} = A\omega B_0\sin\alpha$$

Only the two sides parallel to the axis cut the field lines; the other two contribute nothing.

2. AC Output

Slip rings

$$V_{\mathrm{emf}} = NA\omega B_0\sin(\omega t + C_0)$$

Slip rings keep each end of the coil on its own brush, so the output alternates. A split-ring commutator instead flips the connection every half turn, giving a pulsating DC output.

3. Energy Balance

Lenz at work

$$T\omega = NIAB_0|\sin\alpha|\,\omega = V_{\mathrm{emf}}I$$

The current drawn by the load produces a torque that opposes the rotation: the engine must supply exactly the electrical power delivered.

Interactive Simulation

Rotating Loop with Slip Rings or Commutator

The field $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}B_0$ points from the N pole (bottom) to the S pole (top). The loop turns about the $x$ axis; its normal is $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \hat{\mathbf{y}}\sin\alpha + \hat{\mathbf{z}}\cos\alpha$. In generator mode the speed is set by you and the loop drives a load $R$. In motor mode a DC supply $V_{\mathrm{s}}$ feeds the loop through the commutator; the loop speeds up until its back-emf nearly balances $V_{\mathrm{s}}$. In generator mode, increase speed or field strength and compare the induced voltage. In motor mode, watch acceleration decrease as back-emf rises: back-emf reduces the current available to produce torque.

Derivation

Motional emf of the Rotating Loop

The loop has length $l$ along the axis and width $w$ ($A = lw$). Segments 1–2 and 3–4 move with velocities $\pm\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,\omega w/2$; segments 2–3 and 4–1 give $(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B})\perp d\mathbf{l}$ and contribute nothing.

Step 1

Velocities of the long sides

$$\mathbf{u}_{12} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\,\omega\frac{w}{2},\qquad \mathbf{u}_{34} = -\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,\omega\frac{w}{2}$$

with $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \hat{\mathbf{y}}\sin\alpha + \hat{\mathbf{z}}\cos\alpha$; the two long sides move in opposite directions.

Step 2

Line integral

$$V_{\mathrm{emf}}^{\mathrm{m}} = \int_{-l/2}^{l/2}\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}\omega\tfrac{w}{2}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}}B_0\right]\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}\,dx + \int_{l/2}^{-l/2}\left[-\hat{\mathbf{n}}\omega\tfrac{w}{2}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}}B_0\right]\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}\,dx = A\omega B_0\sin\alpha$$

With $\alpha = \omega t + C_0$ and $N$ turns: $V_{\mathrm{emf}} = NA\omega B_0\sin(\omega t + C_0)$. The same result follows from $-d\Phi/dt$ with $\Phi = NAB_0\cos\alpha$. The flux is largest when the loop normal aligns with the field, but the emf is zero there because the flux is momentarily stationary. Maximum emf occurs when flux changes fastest, not when flux is largest.
