Electrostatics

Electric Potential and Path-Independent Work

Moving a charge $q$ against an electric field requires an external force $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} = -q\mathbf{E}$. The work done per unit charge is the electric potential difference, measured in volts ($\mathrm{J}/\mathrm{C}$). The external-force expression describes slow motion without changing kinetic energy. Potential belongs to the field and its reference choice, whereas potential energy also depends on the sign and amount of charge.

Differential

Potential Change Along $d\mathbf{l}$

$$dV = \frac{dW}{q} = -\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$$

Moving along $\mathbf{E}$ lowers the potential; moving against it raises the potential.

Line Integral

Potential Difference $V_{21}$

$$V_{21} = V_2 - V_1 = -\int_{P_1}^{P_2} \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$$

In electrostatics the result depends only on the end points, not on the path taken.

Conservative Field

Zero Work Around a Loop

$$\oint_C \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0 \iff \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$$

Hence a single-valued $V$ exists, with $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ and $V(\infty) = 0$ as the usual reference.

Reading the Map

  • $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ points downhill, from high to low potential.
  • $\mathbf{E}$ is everywhere perpendicular to the equipotential lines.
  • Closely spaced equipotentials mean a strong field.
  • Moving along an equipotential takes no work.

Continuous Distributions

Replace $q_i$ by $\rho_{\mathrm{v}}\,dv'$, $\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\,ds'$ or $\rho_{\ell}\,dl'$ and integrate:

$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{v'} \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{v}}}{R'}\, dv'$$

A scalar integral: usually far easier than integrating the vector $d\mathbf{E}$, after which $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$.

Electric Dipole: Exact vs. Far-Field

Poisson's and Laplace's Equations

Combining $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_{\mathrm{v}}$ with $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ gives $\nabla^2 V = -\rho_{\mathrm{v}}/\varepsilon$. Between two plates ($d = 1\ \mathrm{cm}$) we solve it in one dimension, both exactly and by numerical relaxation.

$V(0) = V_1$
$V(d) = V_2$
$\rho_{\mathrm{v}}$
Finite differences: $V_i \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{2}\left(V_{i-1} + V_{i+1} + \rho_{\mathrm{v}}\Delta x^2/\varepsilon_0\right)$  ·  iteration 0

Exact Solution

$$\frac{d^2V}{dx^2} = -\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{v}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
$$V(x) = V_1 + (V_2 - V_1)\frac{x}{d} + \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{v}}}{2\varepsilon_0}\,x(d - x)$$
$V$ at mid-gap
$E_x$ at $x = 0$
Laplace ($\rho_{\mathrm{v}} = 0$): $V$ is a straight line and $\mathbf{E}$ is uniform, as in an ideal parallel-plate capacitor.
Poisson ($\rho_{\mathrm{v}} \neq 0$): positive charge makes $V(x)$ bulge upward (concave down); negative charge makes it sag. The curvature is the charge.
