Dielectrics and Polarization

Material Media

Dielectrics: Bound Charges and Polarization

In a dielectric the charges are bound to their atoms. An applied field cannot make them flow, but it can stretch each atom or molecule into a tiny dipole. The sum of these dipoles, the polarization $\mathbf{P}$, modifies the relation between $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{E}$. This is the ideal insulating model: polarization displaces bound charge locally, while conduction transports mobile charge through the material. The distinction explains how a capacitor dielectric can respond strongly without carrying a large leakage current.

Dipole Moment

Induced Dipole

$$\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{d}\quad(\mathrm{C\cdot m})$$

$\mathbf{d}$ points from the centre of the electron cloud to the nucleus. $\mathbf{P}$ is the dipole moment per unit volume.

Flux Density

$\mathbf{D}$ in a Dielectric

$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}$$

Gauss's law relates the divergence of $\mathbf{D}$ to free charge, while $\mathbf{P}$ describes polarization by bound charge. Free charge alone does not generally determine the entire electric flux density field without the geometry and material relations.

Linear Medium

Susceptibility and Permittivity

$$\mathbf{P} = \varepsilon_0\chi_{\mathrm{e}}\mathbf{E},\ \ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}} = 1 + \chi_{\mathrm{e}}$$

Then $\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0(1+\chi_{\mathrm{e}})\mathbf{E} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E}$ with $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}\varepsilon_0$.

Linear: $|\mathbf{P}|$ proportional to $|\mathbf{E}|$.
Isotropic: $\mathbf{P}$ parallel to $\mathbf{E}$ for every direction of $\mathbf{E}$.
Homogeneous: $\varepsilon$ the same at every point. This course assumes all three unless stated.

A Dielectric Slab Between Charged Plates

The plates carry fixed free charge $\pm\rho_{\mathrm{s}}$. Inside the slab, bound charges $\mp\rho_{\mathrm{ps}}$ appear on its faces and partly cancel the free charge: $\mathbf{D}$ is unchanged, $\mathbf{E}$ drops by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}$, and $\mathbf{P}$ makes up the difference. Increase relative permittivity while keeping the free plate charge fixed. The stronger polarization reduces the internal electric field. A fixed-voltage experiment would instead let the source supply additional free charge.

Isotropic vs. Anisotropic Media

In a crystal the susceptibility can differ along its axes, $P_x = \varepsilon_0\chi_x E_x$ and $P_y = \varepsilon_0\chi_y E_y$. Rotate $\mathbf{E}$ and watch $\mathbf{P}$ lag behind its direction. Anisotropy means the response depends on direction, not simply on field strength. Compare the principal-axis settings with an intermediate angle to see why one scalar permittivity is insufficient.
