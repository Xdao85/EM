Gauss's Law: Differential and Integral Forms

Gauss's law states that the total outward flux of the electric flux density $\mathbf{D}$ through any closed surface $S$ equals the total charge $Q$ enclosed by $S$. Charges outside $S$ contribute flux that enters and leaves, adding nothing. Flux is a signed measure of field crossing a surface, not the field magnitude at one point. Gauss's law always holds, but finding the field directly from it requires enough symmetry to simplify the surface integral.

Differential Form

Point by Point

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_{\mathrm{v}}$$

The divergence of $\mathbf{D}$ at a point equals the volume charge density there: flux lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge.

Divergence Theorem

The Bridge

$$\int_v \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D}\, dv = \oint_S \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{s}$$

Integrating the differential form over the volume $v$ bounded by $S$ turns it into a surface integral.

Integral Form

Over a Closed Surface

$$\oint_S \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{s} = Q$$

$d\mathbf{s} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, ds$ points outward. Only the enclosed charge $Q$ appears on the right-hand side.

Why it is powerful: when the charge distribution has spherical, cylindrical or planar symmetry, choose a Gaussian surface on which $\mathbf{D}$ is either parallel to $d\mathbf{s}$ with constant magnitude, or perpendicular to it. Then $\oint_S \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{s}$ reduces to $D \times (\text{area})$ and $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\varepsilon$ follows in one line.

Surface-by-Surface

$$\oint_S \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{s} = \underbrace{\int_{\text{top}}}_{0} + \underbrace{\int_{\text{bottom}}}_{0} + \int_{\text{side}} \hat{\mathbf{r}}D_r\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}\, r\,d\phi\,dz$$

On the caps $d\mathbf{s} = \pm\hat{\mathbf{z}}\,ds$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{D}$. The side gives $2\pi r h D_r = \rho_{\ell} h$, so

$$\mathbf{E} = \hat{\mathbf{r}}\,\frac{\rho_{\ell}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$

Worked Example

Field of a Uniformly Charged Sphere

A sphere of radius $a$ holds a uniform $\rho_{\mathrm{v}}$. Move the Gaussian sphere of radius $R$ inside and outside the charge and watch the enclosed charge set the field. Inside the charged sphere, increasing radius includes more charge; outside, all charge is already enclosed and only the area grows. This explains the different radial trends without memorizing separate formulas.

Sphere radius $a$
$\rho_{\mathrm{v}}$

$$R \le a:\ \ D_R 4\pi R^2 = \rho_{\mathrm{v}}\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \Rightarrow \mathbf{E} = \hat{\mathbf{R}}\,\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{v}} R}{3\varepsilon_0}$$
$$R \ge a:\ \ \mathbf{E} = \hat{\mathbf{R}}\,\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{v}} a^3}{3\varepsilon_0 R^2} = \hat{\mathbf{R}}\,\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}$$

$|\mathbf{E}|$ at $R$

Outside, the sphere acts exactly like a point charge $Q$ at its centre; inside, $E$ grows linearly and peaks at the surface.

Interactive Simulation 4

Symmetry Sets the Decay Rate

The field falls off as fast as the Gaussian surface area grows with distance.

Point charge: sphere, area $4\pi R^2$ ⇒ $E \propto 1/R^2$.
Line charge: cylinder side, area $2\pi r h$ ⇒ $E \propto 1/r$.
Plane of charge: pill box, cap area fixed ⇒ $E = \rho_{\mathrm{s}}/2\varepsilon_0$, independent of distance.

All three curves are normalised to 1 at $r = 1$ so the shapes can be compared directly.
