Conductors and the Point Form of Ohm's Law

A material is described by three constitutive parameters: permittivity $\varepsilon$, permeability $\mu$ and conductivity $\sigma$. The conductivity decides how strongly an applied field drives a current. A good conductor supports substantial current with a small internal electric field, while an insulator supports very little conduction current. Zero internal field is an equilibrium result, not a rule for a resistive wire carrying steady current.

Ohm's Law

Point Form

$$\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E} \quad (\mathrm{A}/\mathrm{m}^2)$$

$\sigma$ in siemens per metre ($\mathrm{S}/\mathrm{m}$). A medium is homogeneous if $\sigma$ is the same everywhere and isotropic if it does not depend on direction.

Perfect Conductor

$\sigma = \infty$

$$\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{J}/\sigma = 0$$

A finite current needs no field, so $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside. In electrostatics this also holds for any real conductor.

Perfect Dielectric

$\sigma = 0$

$$\mathbf{J} = 0$$

No conduction current flows regardless of $\mathbf{E}$. Real insulators have tiny but nonzero $\sigma$.

Why $\mathbf{E} = 0$ Inside a Conductor

Switch on an external field. Free electrons drift against it and pile up on one face, leaving positive ions exposed on the other. The induced surface charges create an opposing field that grows until the net interior field is zero. Watch the external and induced contributions separately: the final cancellation concerns the interior field, while the field outside generally remains nonzero. Charges stop redistributing when electrostatic equilibrium is reached.

Drift Velocity and Mobility

Carriers move randomly at thermal speeds and collide constantly; the field adds a small net drift. Electrons drift against $\mathbf{E}$, holes along it: $\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{e}} = -\mu_{\mathrm{e}}\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{h}} = \mu_{\mathrm{h}}\mathbf{E}$.

$$\mathbf{J} = (N_{\mathrm{e}}\mu_{\mathrm{e}} + N_{\mathrm{h}}\mu_{\mathrm{h}})\,e\,\mathbf{E} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$$

Resistance and Conductance

In general $R = V/I$ with $V = -\int_l \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ and $I = \int_S \sigma\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{s}$. Compare a uniform bar with the radial leakage through the insulation of a coaxial cable. For a uniform wire, increasing length raises resistance, while increasing cross-sectional area lowers it. In the coaxial geometry, the current spreads radially and the local conducting area varies with radius.

Uniform Bar

$\mathbf{E}$ is uniform along the bar, so $V = El$ and $I = \sigma E A$:

$$R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{l}{\sigma A}$$

Coaxial Insulation (radial leakage)

Leakage current $I$ flows radially from the inner to the outer conductor: $\mathbf{J} = \hat{\mathbf{r}}\, I/(2\pi r l)$, so $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{J}/\sigma$ and $V = \frac{I}{2\pi\sigma l}\ln(b/a)$:

$$G' = \frac{G}{l} = \frac{2\pi\sigma}{\ln(b/a)}\quad(\mathrm{S/m})$$

Joule's Law: Power Dissipated as Heat

The field does work on drifting carriers; collisions pass that energy to the lattice as heat. For a wire of length $l$ and cross-section $A$:

$$P = \int_v \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{J}\, dv = \int_v \sigma|\mathbf{E}|^2 dv = I^2 R$$
Current $I$
Diameter $d$
Material ($l = 1$ m)
$R = l/(\sigma A)$
$E = J/\sigma$
Power density $\sigma E^2$
$P = I^2R$

Same current, higher resistance: the thin nichrome wire of a toaster glows while a copper lead stays cool. Halving the diameter quadruples $R$ and therefore $P$.
