Cartesian, Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

A point is the intersection of three coordinate surfaces. In an orthogonal system these surfaces meet at right angles, so the three base vectors at every point are mutually perpendicular and obey the right-hand rule. The price for matching the symmetry of a problem (a wire, a sphere) is that cylindrical and spherical base vectors change direction from point to point. Use Cartesian coordinates for planar geometry, cylindrical coordinates for a long wire, and spherical coordinates for a point source. Matching the symmetry often reduces the number of variables needed in an equation.

Cartesian

Three planes

$$\hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}},\quad dv = dx\,dy\,dz$$

Surfaces $x = x_1$, $y = y_1$, $z = z_1$. Base vectors are the same everywhere.

Cylindrical

Cylinder, half-plane, plane

$$\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\boldsymbol{\hat{\phi}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}},\quad dv = r\,dr\,d\phi\,dz$$

$0 \le r < \infty$, $0 \le \phi < 2\pi$. $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\hat{\phi}}$ depend on $\phi$.

Spherical

Sphere, cone, half-plane

$$\hat{\mathbf{R}}\times\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}} = \boldsymbol{\hat{\phi}},\quad dv = R^2\sin\theta\,dR\,d\theta\,d\phi$$

$0 \le \theta \le \pi$ measured from $+z$; all three base vectors depend on $\theta$ and $\phi$.

Interactive Simulation

3D Coordinate Explorer

Set a point in Cartesian, cylindrical or spherical coordinates — the other two systems follow. The scene shows the components, the coordinate surfaces, the base vectors at the point and the differential element $d\mathbf{l}$, $d\mathbf{s}$, $dv$ of the active system. Drag to orbit, scroll to zoom. Vary one coordinate at a time to identify its coordinate surface. Observe that an angular increment corresponds to a longer physical distance farther from the axis or origin; this explains the scale factors in line, area and volume elements.
