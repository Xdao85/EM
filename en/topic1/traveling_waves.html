Traveling Waves

Imagine a pulse moving along a string: the disturbance travels while each small section of string moves locally. A wave transfers energy without requiring bulk transport of the medium; electromagnetic waves need no material medium at all. Sinusoidal waves can be described through the phase $\omega t - \beta x$, which links position and time. Following a point of constant phase reveals the direction and speed of travel.

1-dimensional

Wave on a string

In a one-dimensional model, the disturbance depends on one spatial coordinate and time, $y(x,t)$. For a transverse wave on a taut string, the displacement is perpendicular to the direction of propagation. The curve shows displacement, not the path followed by a single piece of string.

2-dimensional

Circular surface wave

For an ideal circular wave spreading over a surface, wavefronts are circles. Energy is distributed over a growing circumference, so the amplitude decreases as $1/\sqrt{r}$ far from the source, even without absorption. This geometric spreading is different from material loss.

3-dimensional

Spherical wave

An ideal point source radiates outward with spherical wavefronts. In a lossless medium, energy spreads over an area that grows with distance squared, so amplitude falls as $1/R$. This illustrates far-field spreading from a lamp or antenna; real antennas also have directional radiation patterns.

Sinusoidal Wave in a Lossless Medium

This one-dimensional lossless wave keeps its amplitude as it travels. The upper plot is a spatial snapshot; drag the amber probe to choose $x_0$, and the lower plot records $y(x_0, t)$ for a stationary observer. Wavelength controls the spacing between crests, frequency controls how often crests pass the probe, and phase shifts the pattern. Reverse the direction and follow one crest to distinguish propagation from local oscillation.

$$y(x,t) = A\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T} \mp \frac{2\pi x}{\lambda} + \phi_0\right) = A\cos(\omega t \mp \beta x + \phi_0)$$

Sinusoidal Wave in a Lossy Medium

In a lossy medium, energy is dissipated, often as heat, and the amplitude envelope decreases as $e^{-\alpha x}$. The attenuation constant $\alpha$, measured in nepers per meter, describes the rate of decay with distance. Increase it to see the same initial wave fade more rapidly; set it to zero to recover the lossless case. This envelope represents absorption rather than geometric spreading.

$$y(x,t) = A\,e^{-\alpha x}\cos(\omega t - \beta x + \phi_0)$$

At $x = \delta = 1/\alpha$, the amplitude is $e^{-1} \approx 37\%$ of its starting value. In a good conductor, this characteristic penetration distance is the skin depth $\delta_s$. A stronger attenuation gives a shorter penetration distance, which helps explain why high-frequency fields are concentrated near conductor surfaces.

For a wave with fixed impedance, power is proportional to amplitude squared and therefore decays as $e^{-2\alpha x}$. An amplitude attenuation of $1\ \mathrm{Np} = 8.686\ \mathrm{dB}$ leaves about 37% of the amplitude but only about 14% of the power. Distinguish amplitude reduction from power reduction when interpreting transmission loss.

In this simulation, changing attenuation changes only the envelope because frequency and wavelength are independent controls. In a real material, loss and dispersion can also change the propagation constant and wavelength; the frequency of a steady wave remains set by its source.
