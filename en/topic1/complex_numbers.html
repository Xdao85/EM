Review of Complex Numbers

Core representation

Rectangular and Polar Forms

Imagine a sinusoidal voltage as the projection of a rotating arrow. At a known frequency, one complex number records its amplitude and phase, making AC circuits and electromagnetic waves easier to analyse. Engineers use $j = \sqrt{-1}$ for the imaginary unit because the letter $i$ commonly denotes current.

Rectangular form
$$z = x + jy$$

$x = \mathfrak{Re}(z)$ is the horizontal coordinate (real part), and $y = \mathfrak{Im}(z)$ is the vertical coordinate (imaginary part). These are two real-valued components of one complex number. Addition and subtraction combine the corresponding components.

Polar form
$$z = |z|e^{j\theta} = |z|\angle\theta$$

$|z| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ is the distance from the origin, and $\theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$ describes the angle from the positive real axis. Choose the correct quadrant (add $180^\circ$ when $x<0$, then use an equivalent angle in the desired range); when x is zero, use the direction of the imaginary axis. The angle at the origin is undefined. The projections are $x = |z|\cos\theta$, $y = |z|\sin\theta$. Polar form makes products, quotients and powers easier to interpret.

Complex conjugate
$$z^* = x - jy = |z|e^{-j\theta}$$

Conjugation reflects the point across the real axis: the magnitude stays the same and the angle changes sign. Multiplying a number by its conjugate gives a real, nonnegative result, so $|z| = \sqrt{z\,z^*}$. This operation also appears in AC power calculations.

$j = e^{j\pi/2} = 1\angle 90^\circ$
$-1 = e^{j\pi} = 1\angle 180^\circ$
$\dfrac{1}{j} = -j = 1\angle -90^\circ$
$\sqrt{j} = \pm\dfrac{1+j}{\sqrt{2}}$

Euler's Identity and the Rotating Phasor

Imagine an arrow of fixed length rotating at a constant speed. Euler's identity $e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta$ connects its angle to its horizontal and vertical projections. For $z(t) = A\,e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}$, these projections are $\mathfrak{Re}\{z\} = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ and $\mathfrak{Im}\{z\} = A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$. Increase A to enlarge both traces, increase the angular frequency to shorten their period, and change the initial phase to shift their timing.

Time runs left→right; the newest value is at the phasor.

Why this matters

A real sinusoidal signal can be recovered from the real part of a rotating complex number. At $t = 0$, or after factoring out the common time dependence, the remaining number is $A e^{j\phi}$, the phasor: it stores amplitude and phase for a specified frequency. Differentiation of the rotating exponential becomes multiplication by $j\omega$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\,e^{j(\omega t+\phi)} = j\omega\,e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}$$

Multiplication by $j$ rotates the arrow by $90^\circ$; the angular frequency also scales the derivative's amplitude. Thus the derivative of $\cos$ is $-\sin$, which leads the original cosine by a quarter period. This is why phasors turn time derivatives in sinusoidal steady-state circuit and field equations into algebraic operations.
